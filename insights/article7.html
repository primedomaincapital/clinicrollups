The Earnout That Never Pays: How Rollup Deals Structure the Second Check

Ten percent of the price, paid over two years if the numbers hold. The numbers are measured by the buyer, after the buyer takes control — and the accounting decides what you get.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The offer was $1.96 million, and $196,000 of it never reached the closing table. That was the earnout: 10% of the price, paid over two years if the practice hit its EBITDA targets. Two years later the seller received half of it, and the explanation arrived in a spreadsheet. The practice had produced the same patients, the same production, the same team, and $392,000 of earnings before any corporate charges, in both years of the earnout. The earnout paid $98,000 a year in full at $352,000 of practice EBITDA, nothing below $312,000, and on a straight line in between. The only thing that changed was how the buyer counted its overhead. That is the earnout in a sentence: a bet on a number you will no longer control, measured by the party who benefits when the number comes in low.

An earnout is a contractual mechanism that ties a portion of the purchase price to the future performance of the practice after closing. Instead of receiving the full agreed value at closing, the seller receives a base payment upfront and earns additional compensation over a defined period — typically one to three years — if the practice hits specific targets. The provision defines four things: the metric, usually gross collections, net revenue, EBITDA, or patient visit volume; the measurement period, often one, two, or three years after closing; the payout structure, binary or sliding scale, with a floor and a ceiling; and the payment timeline, usually annual or at the end of the measurement period. In the dental example above, the arithmetic is exact enough to be instructive. If the practice is charged only for the services it actually uses — say $24,000 a year for billing, payroll, and IT — it reports $368,000 and the full $196,000 is paid. If the buyer charges a management fee of $60,000 a year, the practice reports $332,000 and half, $98,000, is paid. If the buyer allocates a share of its corporate overhead — the regional managers, the marketing, the executive team — of $96,000 a year, the practice reports $296,000 and nothing is paid. Same practice, same production, same two years: $196,000, $98,000, or zero, depending entirely on the accounting.

The mechanism is not an accident and it is not rare. Many earnouts never pay the full amount and some do not pay out at all, because the business fails to meet the agreed targets — and the biggest problem is that the seller is still taking the risk even though the seller is no longer in control. Once the sale closes, the buyer owns the practice and makes the key decisions: who to hire, how much to spend on marketing, what to charge, where to cut costs. If the buyer reduces the marketing budget or changes staff compensation, revenue may fall. If the best associate leaves because of those changes, the practice may miss its targets. The seller could lose part or all of the earnout even though the seller had no control over the decisions that caused the problem. The further the metric sits from production, the less of the result is the seller's to move: a collections-based earnout is hard for a buyer to reduce through accounting, because collections are money in the door. An EBITDA-based earnout is the most vulnerable, because EBITDA is what remains after the buyer decides what to charge against it — and the purchase agreement usually does not define what can be charged.

The structure has variations that matter more than the headline percentage. A binary earnout pays everything if the target is hit and nothing if it is missed — a cliff that turns a $1,000 shortfall into a total loss. A sliding scale pays in proportion to attainment, which is fairer but still depends on where the floor and the ceiling sit. Clawbacks and set-offs let some buyers take back earnout already paid if a later period falls short, or withhold payments against any claim the buyer makes under the purchase agreement. Acceleration clauses matter in the other direction: if the platform is sold, if the seller is let go without cause, or if the buyer breaks its operating promises, the unearned earnout should become payable in full — because without that clause, a new owner can measure the seller on a number it has no reason to protect. Dispute mechanics matter too: the seller should receive the calculation and the supporting statements within a set number of days after each period, have the right to review the books behind them, and have disagreements settled by an independent accountant on a fixed timeline, not by the buyer's controller.

The trade most sellers actually make is not about the earnout's terms at all. It is about its size. Many practice owners try to negotiate a higher guaranteed payment at closing instead of relying on an earnout — trading a smaller contingent piece for more cash on the day the deal closes. The math favors the trade more often than sellers expect: a 10% earnout that pays half is worth 5% of the price, and a seller who gives up that 10% earnout in exchange for five more points of cash at close has taken the same expected value with none of the risk. The buyer will resist, because the earnout is also the buyer's protection — it is the part of the price the buyer does not have to fund if the practice underperforms, and it keeps the seller engaged through the transition. The negotiation is therefore a straight trade of risk for certainty, and the seller who understands that the earnout is a discount the buyer has already applied to the price is the seller who negotiates from the right side of it.

The counterargument is that the earnout exists for a real reason, and in 2026 it is becoming more common rather than less. Buyers are more cautious than they were during the acquisition frenzy of 2020 and 2021, and the groups still active in the market are moving more carefully — which means sellers should expect to see earnout provisions more frequently and with more structural complexity than in prior deal cycles. The earnout bridges a genuine valuation gap: the buyer will not pay today for growth the seller expects tomorrow, and the seller will not accept today's price for a practice the seller believes is worth more. Structured well, the earnout lets both sides be right. The other counterargument is that the metric choice solves most of the problem: a revenue-based earnout is usually a better option than a profit-based one, because revenue is much harder for a buyer to reduce through accounting decisions or additional overhead. The seller who insists on collections or gross revenue as the metric, with the accounting definitions written into the agreement, has removed most of the buyer's ability to move the number.

The practical move is to negotiate the earnout as if it were the whole deal, because for 10% to 30% of the price, it is. Insist on a metric the seller can still influence after closing — collections or revenue, not EBITDA. Insist that the agreement define what can be charged against the number: direct services at cost, no corporate overhead allocation, no management fee above a stated amount. Insist that each period stand on its own, with set-off only against claims that are agreed or decided. Insist on acceleration if the platform is sold or the seller is terminated without cause, and on audit rights with a fixed timeline and an independent accountant for disputes. And price the earnout at zero when comparing offers — because the cash at close, typically 70% to 80% of the price, is the only part of the deal that is certain, and two offers with the same headline price can put very different amounts of money in the seller's bank. The earnout that never pays is not a surprise. It is a structure. The seller's job is to read the structure before signing it.
