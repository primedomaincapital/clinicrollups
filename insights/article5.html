The Platform Premium: What the First Clinic in a Rollup Gets Paid

The same $2 million of earnings sells for $10 million as an add-on — and $20 million as a platform. The difference is not the clinic. It is the position.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

Two gastroenterology groups, each earning $2 million a year, sold in the same quarter. One sold as an add-on to an existing private equity platform and fetched $10 million. The other sold as the platform itself — the first acquisition in a new rollup — and fetched $20 million. Same specialty, same earnings, same quarter, double the price. The difference was not the medicine. It was the position: the platform seller was selling the story the fund would tell its next investors, and the add-on seller was selling a line item in a story someone else had already written. That gap — three to five turns of EBITDA, in the language of the deal — is the most important number in the clinic rollup market, and it is the reason the first clinic in a rollup gets the best price in the whole sequence.

The gap is not a rounding error and it is not the same in every specialty. Compiled from 2024 and 2025 transactions by FOCUS Investment Banking, Levin Associates, and PitchBook, the platform and add-on ranges run like this: ophthalmology platforms price at 12 to 20 times EBITDA against 7 to 11 times for add-ons, a gap of seven turns. Oncology and urology platforms price at 14 to 19 times against 8 to 12 times for add-ons, a gap of six and a half. Primary care platforms price at 8 to 12 times against 3 to 6 times for add-ons, a gap of five and a half. Cardiology platforms run 12 to 15 times against 8 to 12 times for add-ons. Gastroenterology runs 10 to 14 times against 7 to 9 times. Orthopedics runs 9 to 13 times against 6 to 9 times. The pattern is consistent: the platform commands three to five turns more than the add-on in the same specialty, and the gap is widest where the specialty is scarcest or the ancillary revenue is richest. The median healthcare services multiple across the market was about 11.5 times EBITDA in 2025, down from 14.5 times in 2024 — a reminder that the platform premium is measured against a moving market, not a fixed one.

The premium has a mechanism, and it is worth understanding because it explains both the price and the catch. A private equity fund buys one large group as the platform — typically $3 million or more in EBITDA — then folds smaller practices into it as add-ons, typically $1 million to $5 million in EBITDA, and tuck-ins under $1 million. The fund centralizes billing, contracting, and back-office functions, negotiates better payer contracts through scale, and eventually sells the combined business at the platform multiple rather than the add-on multiple. The arithmetic is the engine: buy independent dermatology practices at 4 to 6 times EBITDA, assemble a $50 million EBITDA platform, and sell it at 10 to 14 times. The value is created mostly by aggregation rather than by any improvement in clinical care. The first seller gets the best price because the fund needs the platform to exist before any of the rest of the arithmetic works — and because the first seller is usually asked to stay on as chief medical officer or regional lead, lending the fund a brand, a referral network, and a management team it does not yet have.

The structure of the deal is where the headline multiple meets reality. A typical deal pays 70% to 80% of the price in cash at close and 20% to 30% as rollover equity — an investment in the platform company that is illiquid until the fund exits, usually in three to seven years. On a $20 million platform sale at 10 times, that means $14 million to $16 million in cash and $4 million to $6 million in rollover. The rollover is where the real money is, in the pitch: a well-run platform that exits to a larger fund at a higher multiple in year four can turn a $3.5 million rollover into $7 million to $10 million. It is also where the real risk is, because the rollover is only worth something if the platform exits, and the exit multiple is not guaranteed — the median multiple fell three turns in a single year. Ancillary revenue moves the number further: owned ambulatory surgery centers, imaging, and pathology commonly add one to three turns to the multiple, and practices with strong payer diversification and ancillary revenue achieve multiples up to twice those of comparable practices without them. The platform premium is real, but it is paid partly in paper and partly in promises.

The deal volume behind the premium is still running hot, even as buyers get more selective. Global healthcare private equity delivered a record year in 2025, with disclosed deal value exceeding an estimated $191 billion and surpassing the previous high set in 2021, on 445 buyouts. The first quarter of 2025 saw 79 physician practice deals, and overall volume held in the mid-260s to low-300s per quarter through the year, according to PwC. Deal value moved around: $19 billion in the fourth quarter of 2024, down to $7 billion in the third quarter of 2025, back to $22 billion in the fourth. The marquee transactions set the top of the range: TPG and AmerisourceBergen's OneOncology deal included a 19-times EBITDA put and call, and Cencora's acquisition of Retina Consultants of America underscored high-teens retina platform pricing. The specialties most actively targeted in 2026 are dermatology, gastroenterology, ophthalmology, orthopedics, urology, primary care, and behavioral health — and in each of them, the fund is looking for the same thing: a platform-sized group in an underconsolidated market, with the ancillaries and the payer contracts that justify the top of the range.

The counterargument is that the platform premium is the most expensive money a clinic owner will ever take. The platform seller gets the highest multiple, the longest hold, the largest rollover, and the most operational involvement after closing. The add-on seller gets a lower multiple but a faster process, narrower diligence, and a smaller rollover — and inherits the platform's systems, billing, and compensation grid, which is a cost as well as a benefit. The tuck-in seller gets the lowest multiple and the least say. There is also the matter of what the premium is actually buying: the fund is paying for the right to use the seller's name, network, and management team to assemble the rollup, and the seller is being paid in a currency — rollover equity — whose value depends entirely on the fund's ability to sell the platform later at a higher multiple. When the median multiple falls from 14.5 to 11.5 times in a year, that dependency is not theoretical. The platform premium is the best price in the market and the most contingent one, in the same deal.

The implication for a clinic owner weighing a sale is to price the position, not just the earnings. If the practice is platform-sized — $3 million or more in EBITDA, multi-site, with ancillaries and payer diversification — the first-seller premium is worth negotiating hard for, because it is the only time in the rollup sequence that the seller holds the leverage. If the practice is add-on-sized, the question is different: is the add-on price, plus the rollover, plus the second bite at the platform exit, worth more than a peer sale or waiting? The answer turns on the rollover terms, the exit timeline, and the platform's actual pipeline — not on the headline multiple, which is the number the fund most wants the seller to focus on. The first clinic in a rollup gets paid for the story. The seller's job is to make sure the story is written into the agreement, not just the pitch deck.
