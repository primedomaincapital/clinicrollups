How Long a Clinic Sale Takes, from LOI to Close

Six to nine months is the standard answer. Twelve to twenty-four is the honest one — and the slowest step is almost always the seller's own paperwork.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The letter of intent arrived in March, with a price the owner had not expected and a 90-day exclusivity window that felt generous. By June the exclusivity had expired, diligence was still open, and the buyer's attorney was asking for the third year of tax returns the seller's CPA had promised in April. By September the deal closed — six months after the LOI, nine months after the first conversation, and two years after the owner had first thought about selling. The owner called it a fast sale. The buyer called it normal. Both were right, because the clock on a clinic sale runs on three different calendars at once: the deal calendar, the regulatory calendar, and the seller's own calendar. The deal calendar says six to nine months. The regulatory calendar says whatever it says. The seller's calendar is the one that decides which of the other two actually happens.

The standard answer, from the firms that run these sales, is six to nine months from first consultation to closing. The process has nine steps: consultation, valuation and documentation, confidential marketing, non-disclosure agreements, buyer engagement, a letter of intent, due diligence, legal and regulatory approvals, and closing. Once a mandate is live, sell-side processes in physician practice typically run six to ten months from intake to close. The longer answer, from the American College of Surgeons and the transaction guides, is twelve to twenty-four months from preparation to close — and the preparation is the part most sellers skip. The owner of a healthcare practice should start preparing two to three years before a sale, according to the advisory firms, because the financial cleanup alone takes eighteen to twenty-four months: three to five years of financial statements, profit and loss statements, balance sheets, and tax returns, organized and normalized, before a buyer ever sees them. The sale takes six months. Getting ready to sell takes two years. Sellers who confuse the two are the ones whose deals take eighteen months.

The letter of intent is the hinge of the whole timeline. An LOI sets out the price, the deal structure, the financing, the working capital, the seller transition, the exclusivity, the diligence scope, and the closing conditions — and signing one usually starts an exclusivity period of 90 to 120 days, during which the seller stops talking to other buyers. That exclusivity window is the deal's real deadline: everything after the LOI has to fit inside it, or the seller has to ask for an extension from a buyer who now knows the seller has nowhere else to go. Due diligence runs roughly 90 to 120 days inside that window, and it is where most deals slow down: the buyer verifies financials, clinical operations, compliance, contracts, and legal records, and the slowest step is almost always how quickly the seller can produce the diligence items. Tax returns, profit-and-loss statements, balance sheets, payroll and provider compensation, payer mix, production and collections reports, accounts receivable, leases, licenses, contracts, and compliance records — the package is long, and having it ready early is the single biggest time saver in the whole process. In San Diego, typical diligence runs 60 to 120 days, and coding audits, payer contract review, and credentialing transfers drive most of it.

The regulatory steps are the ones the seller cannot speed up by working harder. Regulatory approvals alone consume 60 to 120 days, according to the California transaction guides, and they run in parallel with diligence rather than after it — which is why the attorneys have to be hired before the LOI, not after. Medicare-certified entities require a change-of-ownership filing, most states require licensing approvals or notifications, and the purchase agreement has to survive Stark Law fair-market-value review, which is what pushes hospital acquisitions toward the 24-month end of the range. Then there is credentialing, which is a cash-flow event disguised as paperwork: commercial payer credentialing runs roughly 90 to 120 days per payer, and it often continues past the closing date, which means the buyer is paying for the practice before the practice can bill at the buyer's rates. A seller who has not started credentialing transfers before closing has built a gap into the buyer's first quarter of ownership — and the buyer will price that gap into the deal.

Delay has a price, and it is not just the calendar. A rate lock on the buyer's financing typically runs 60 to 90 days, and an extension costs money and leverage at the same time. An exclusivity extension costs the seller the only thing the LOI gave them — the ability to talk to other buyers — and the buyer knows it. The deals that stretch past nine months usually stretch because one of three things happened: the diligence package was incomplete, the regulatory filing was started late, or the purchase agreement had to be renegotiated after diligence found something the LOI had not priced. Each of those is preventable, and each of them is more expensive the later it surfaces. A coding audit that finds $200,000 of uncollected revenue in month two is a renegotiation. The same finding in month five, after exclusivity has expired and the seller has turned away other buyers, is a price cut the seller cannot refuse.

The counterargument is that the timeline is not the buyer's fault, and the sellers who complain loudest about it are usually the ones who caused it. A practice with clean books, realistic pricing, and SBA pre-qualification closes in three to nine months from listing. A practice with three years of unreconciled financials, a lease that cannot be assigned, and a compliance audit that has never been done takes twelve to twenty-four months — and the extra year is not the market's doing. The other counterargument runs the other way: the fastest path is also the cheapest one. A tuck-in sale to an existing platform — sub-$1 million in EBITDA, a single location, a geographic hole to fill — closes fastest because there is least to diligence, and it prices at the lowest multiple in the market. Speed and price trade against each other at every step, and the seller who optimizes for speed alone usually discovers the trade too late.

The retirement math is what makes the timeline matter now rather than later. Between 65% and 75% of medical practice sales are driven by retirement, according to U.S. Bank, and physicians aged 55 and older now represent 42% of the clinical workforce. That is a large cohort of owners approaching the same decision at the same time, and the ones who start preparing two years out will sell into a market with buyers competing for platform assets. The ones who wait until they are ready to retire will sell into a market where the buyer knows the seller's timeline — and the buyer's leverage grows with every month the seller needs to close. The sale itself involves five or more professional advisors — a healthcare transactional attorney, a CPA with healthcare M&A experience, a broker specializing in medical practices, and often a valuation firm and a tax advisor — and each of them needs time the seller has to give them.

The practical move is to start the clock eighteen to twenty-four months before the target close date, and to treat the diligence package as the timeline. Assemble three to five years of financials, normalize the owner add-backs, commission a formal valuation, and hire the healthcare transactional attorney and the CPA before the buyer outreach begins. When the LOI arrives, the 90-to-120-day exclusivity window should be spent confirming what the buyer already believes, not discovering what the seller has not yet organized. A clinic sale that takes six months from LOI to close is not a fast sale. It is a prepared one — and the preparation is the only part of the timeline the seller actually controls.
