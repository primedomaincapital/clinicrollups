Roll-Up 2.0: Integration Is the Multiple Now

The buy-and-stack playbook hit its limit. Nearly a third of private-equity healthcare assets are seven years old or more, regulators have three theories aimed at the same structure, and buyers now pay for integration, not aggregation.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 9, 2026 · 8 min read

For years the playbook was simple: buy a platform practice in a specialty, add smaller practices to it, and sell the combined business at a higher multiple than any of its parts would have fetched alone. That playbook has reached its limit. According to data cited from Bain & Company, nearly 30% of private-equity-backed healthcare assets are now aged seven years or older, while another 37% fall in the four-to-six-year range. The traditional three-to-five-year hold is increasingly a relic. Buyers are wary of “bags of bolts” — collections of assets cobbled together without true integration — and the premium now goes to platforms that can show a unified operating model, not a longer list of acquisitions.

The consolidation itself is not in doubt. In 2024, only 42.2% of U.S. physicians worked in practices wholly owned by physicians, down from 60.1% in 2012, according to the American Medical Association’s biennial Physician Practice Benchmark Survey. Private equity owned 6.5% of physicians in 2024, up from about 4.5% in both 2020 and 2022. The deal count tells the same story: private-equity acquisitions of physician practices rose from 75 a year in 2012 to 484 a year in 2021, a six-fold increase, and corporate buyers — private equity firms, insurers, and pharmacies — purchased nearly twice as many physician-owned practices as hospital systems did in 2024 and 2025. Dermatology, gastroenterology, ophthalmology, urology, anesthesiology, and radiology have all seen significant consolidation. A 2023 report by the American Antitrust Institute found that a single private equity firm controlled more than 30% of the market for full-time-equivalent physicians in a given specialty in 28% of metropolitan areas nationwide, and more than 50% in 13%.

Regulators have caught up, and they have not one theory but three, all aimed at the same corporate structure. The first is fraud: the Justice Department declined to prosecute Campus Eye Management Holdings of Hamilton, New Jersey, while separately indicting its founder, in a case built on an Anti-Kickback Statute theory that a management service organization’s revenue was tied to referral volume it could not legally control. The second is control: California’s case against Carbon Health Technologies is a corporate-practice-of-medicine case, alleging not that money changed hands improperly but that the management company exercised control a management company was never supposed to have. The third is market power: the Federal Trade Commission’s case against U.S. Anesthesia Partners and its backer Welsh, Carson, Anderson & Stowe alleges that a string of small, individually unremarkable acquisitions added up to illegal market power. USAP and the FTC reached an agreement in principle on April 23, a federal court paused the case on May 26 while USAP implements the required relief over 180 days, and the terms remain confidential. The FTC finalized a consent order with Welsh Carson in January 2025, requiring the firm to limit its involvement with USAP and notify the FTC of specified future acquisitions. In February 2024 the Colorado attorney general resolved alleged anticompetitive practices by USAP, with the company agreeing to pay $200,000 and divest its exclusive contracts at five Colorado hospitals.

The Texas numbers explain why the FTC acted. After USAP acquired most large anesthesia practices in Houston and Dallas, commercial anesthesia prices increased 18% within six months and nearly 30% within two years, according to a study from the University of Chicago’s Booth School of Business. The FTC said in court filings that USAP’s own market-share data showed many acquisitions resulted in shares above 30% and Herfindahl-Hirschman Index increases over 100, meeting the antitrust thresholds. Many of the deals never faced premerger review at all: they fell below the $126.4 million threshold that triggers Hart-Scott-Rodino filing, letting serial tuck-in acquisitions proceed largely unreported. Around 20% of anesthesia practices had been acquired through private-equity buyouts by 2020, and about 33% of anesthesiologists absorbed; by 2024 the share of practices had grown to about 33%.

The states have added a second layer. California, Indiana, Illinois, Maine, Massachusetts, New Mexico, Rhode Island, and Washington have enacted laws requiring notification before a healthcare transaction can close, with notice windows of 90 days and up to 180 days in some West Coast jurisdictions. California’s AB 1415 requires 90 days’ notice for many private-equity and management-service-organization transactions, and Oregon’s corporate-practice limits took effect in January 2026. Similar notice-and-approval bills advanced in Hawaii, Indiana, New York, Pennsylvania, and Vermont. A roll-up that once closed in weeks now budgets a quarter for notice alone.

The honest version is that the roll-up is not dead. Deal activity continued into 2025 and 2026, if at a more measured pace than the boom years of 2021 through 2023: Partners Group-backed Forefront Dermatology acquired Wichita Dermatology & Aesthetics, Kohlberg & Company-owned United Digestive bought two gastroenterology practices in Georgia and Florida, and Cardinal Health expanded its Specialty Alliance management-services arm to roughly 3,000 gastroenterology and urology providers. Capital continues to flow into behavioral health, fertility care, dermatology, and dental service organizations, while primary-care roll-ups have cooled. And a Texas court’s dismissal of the FTC’s lawsuit has emboldened firms pursuing the same strategy. The market has not rejected consolidation. It has repriced it: the multiple now belongs to the platform that integrated — one set of clinical protocols, one administrative workflow, one technology stack, one clean management structure — and not to the one that merely accumulated.
